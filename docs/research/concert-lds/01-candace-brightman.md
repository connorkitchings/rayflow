# Candace Brightman

Candace Brightman (born 1944) was the Grateful Dead's lighting director, widely described as the band's first touring lighting designer. She began with the band on the Europe '72 tour, stayed through Jerry Garcia's death in 1995 (with a short break), and returned from retirement to light the 2015 Fare Thee Well shows [1][2][5]. She was among the first women in the concert industry in that role and is credited as an influence by later designers such as Chris Kuroda [1][3].

## Verified facts

- Brightman was born in 1944, raised in the Chicago suburbs, and studied set design at St. John's College in Annapolis before moving to New York [5].
- She started at the Anderson Theater in New York, then became house lighting director at Bill Graham's Fillmore East [1][5].
- She lit the Dead at the Anderson, the Fillmore East and their 1971 Capitol Theatre (Port Chester, NY) run; Garcia invited her to join after that run [1][5].
- Her first tour with the band was Europe '72 [1][5].
- In 1982, after Garcia asked her to return, she hired the company Morpheus; Danny English of that company became her production electrician and light operator [1].
- She later worked on Dead-related projects and with Widespread Panic (2004-2008), and in 2015 came out of retirement in Hawaii to light Fare Thee Well [5].
- She moved her dimmers and controller from backstage into the orchestra pit while working with Traffic; she says this changed how she saw the show [2].
- By the 1980s she had tired of fixed lights; moving lights let her light the audience and band as one, which she says she had always wanted to do [2].
- Most theater designers turned a moving light off while repositioning it; she began leaving lights on during moves. The technique came from an accident when crew chief Dan English opened shutters mid-move during "Terrapin Station," and the audience responded well [2].
- Her style used slow, deliberate movement and bold color combinations, including large slow sweeps over the audience; Kuroda cited her sweeps as an influence [2].
- Dan Healy said she built the mood across a night, often having the crowd fully in by about two-thirds through, and avoided gimmicky effects [1].
- Healy said she was one of the few people using moving lights early on, using them for movement spot operators could not provide; she also kept several spot operators [1].
- Dennis McNally said she persuaded the band to fund costlier Panaspots, which could move and change color [1].
- Danny English said she favored UV-style light balanced by pink from the opposite side at various angles, rather than the common red/blue/amber/green washes [1].
- Healy recalled a Madison Square Garden show where she turned the stage blood red, then to icy tones within a moment during a slow song [1].
- In arenas she hung drapes and used lighting to make rooms feel like fantasy settings [1].
- She arrived early, programmed for hours, changed her designs nightly, and Healy said she often wrote cues on the spot [1].
- She and English used headsets during shows and built presets by day to test ideas; she changed the truss shape and hang each year [1].
- In the early years, having all moving lights working at the end of a show was a notable success; crew sometimes switched off matching fixtures to hide failures, which her asymmetrical designs made easier [1].
- Dennis McNally described sensors she attached to musicians' belts so lights could follow them [1].
- She said of the band at Fare Thee Well, "There was no predicting how the band was going to play most of the songs" [3].
- At Fare Thee Well (Soldier Field, Chicago, July 3-5, 2015) she operated the console; Paul Hoffman programmed; Bandit Lites supplied over 500 fixtures including 50 Robe BMFL, 50 MAC Viper, 60 VL3000 Spot, 36 VL3500 Wash, and 2 grandMA2 full-size consoles [3].
- That 2015 design had no set upstage, had to keep sightlines clear for audience behind the stage, and aimed the BMFLs inward at a 30-foot scrim used as a projection surface [3].
- Hoffman described her operating the console "like another musical instrument" alongside the band's improvisation [3].
- She was featured in episode 7 of The Light Side podcast (host Luke Stratton) about her 40+ year career [4].

## Signature looks

- Slow beams rising from knee height to face height, then sweeping over the crowd, so the audience feels inside the show (Waful reporting Fenton Williams' description) [2].
- Beams that stay lit while they travel, so the audience sees the light move rather than snap between positions [2].
- Sudden big color swings on a slow song, such as deep red to icy cool [1].
- Unexpected color pairings: UV-ish light countered by pink from the opposite side instead of stock primary washes [1].
- A show that builds gradually so the room is fully engaged around two-thirds through, with no gimmick effects [1].
- Asymmetrical, changing truss and fixture layouts each year, so rigs never looked identical (inferred from [1]).

## Copyable on a small rig

1. Leave moving heads on while repositioning [2]: do not blackout between position presets; fade intensity up first, then move at slow speed, with the shutter/dimmer open.
2. Slow audience sweeps [2]: program one head chase that starts low pointing at the crowd and tilts up over several bars at very low speed; use one or two heads, not all four.
3. Complementary opposed color [1]: set LED washes on stage-left to UV/blue and stage-right to pink, so opposite sides contrast instead of a uniform wash.
4. Hard color swing on a slow section [1]: store two looks (deep red, then icy white-blue) on a cue or executor and cut/crossfade quickly at a song's quiet moment.
5. Build across the night [1]: start with few fixtures and low intensity, adding heads and wash intensity over the set so the peak lands about two-thirds through.
