# Chris Kuroda

Chris Kuroda is the lighting designer for the band Phish. He first took over the lighting board at a 1989 New Hampshire show and was later asked by guitarist Trey Anastasio to become the band's designer [7]. The New York Times counted about 1,752 Phish shows lit by him since 1989, all but three [1]. He is credited as creative lead and board operator, with Andrew Giffin (hired as programmer in 2009, now associate designer) building ideas and running the moving-truss automation [2][4].

## Verified facts

- Kuroda started on the Phish board on 7 April 1989 in New Hampshire, when the previous lighting designer handed it to him during one song; Anastasio later asked him to take the job [7].
- He has lit roughly 1,752 Phish shows, all but three since 1989 [1].
- A 2023 profile counted 302 lights, some on 30 movable truss pieces, plus 144 "tetras" (bars on the trusses that light, spin and form patterns) [1].
- He controls about 100 base looks and 79 preprogrammed colors, and can change each look's colors, speed, direction and motion [1].
- Phish has no set list, so he often improvises cues live ("punts"); about 30 songs with complex structures have preprogrammed cues triggered with a "GoTo" button, such as "Fluffhead" [1].
- Quote from Kuroda: "We don't punt just because we want to... It's the only way to light this band." [1]
- He favors symmetry, avoids audience blinders ("blasters"), uses stationary lights for texture, and aims for an organic rather than digital look [1].
- For Phish there is no fixed cue order: any cue can follow any other, and lighting is not choreographed to particular songs or song sections; pop tours he has done (Justin Bieber) use fixed cue orders of over 2,700 cues [7].
- He has said he traditionally builds large symmetrical designs for Phish that fans can draw from at any moment, in contrast to start-to-finish structured pop shows [6].
- He described Candace Brightman's Grateful Dead work as a "skeletal base" he built on [8].
- He favored large, slow sweeps through the audience during choruses, layering complementary colors [8].
- Phish's earlier approach used no lasers or video panels [8].
- The 2018 rig concept used about 30 sticks of moving truss forming shapes that change through the night [8]; the truss is run from the lighting console and other computers [7].
- A 2016 rig added LED video panels (78 screens in an overhead structure about 22 feet above the stage, built by TAIT) [5]; the video wall was used for about a year and then dropped as a poor fit [2][7].
- Moving trusses came around 2017 and are now the core of the show; LED bars on the trusses returned in 2021 and 2022 [2].
- On a 2010-dated summer tour (page updated 2016), he ran the show from one grandMA2 console while Giffin made adjustments from a second; Kuroda split the desk between an on-the-fly mode following the music and a cue stack [3].
- In 2022 both Kuroda and Giffin used networked grandMA3 consoles; Kuroda mainly handles lighting and Giffin handles automation triggered through the console [4].
- The 2022 tour rig included 72 Robe Tetra2 LED bars on trusses, 60 Robe Spiiders (44 flown, 16 on deck), and 12 more Tetra2 on deck, on 30 truss sticks flown by Kinesys Apex motors under TAIT Navigator control [4].
- Giffin can add new cues mid-show over the network between consoles, and the team builds looks in a lighting simulator before tour, crediting "dirty" and layered colors with widening the palette beyond primaries [2].
- Before a tour, Kuroda and Giffin spend about five weeks together programming in a studio with consoles and 3D lighting software [2].

## Signature looks

- Matching left and right sides, so the stage looks balanced (symmetry; [1][6][8]).
- Slow wide beam sweeps across the crowd on big choruses, in two colors that complement each other (inferred from [8]).
- Steady, non-moving lights used as texture behind moving ones (inferred from [1]).
- The roof structure itself changing shape through the night as trusses move (from [7][8]).
- Looks that change fast and in any order, since cues are not tied to song sections (from [7]).
- Layered, slightly muddy or "dirty" colors rather than only pure primaries (from [2]).

## Copyable on a small rig

1. Mirror your fixtures: put 2 heads and 4 washes per side with identical groups and color, so every look is symmetrical. Source [1].
2. Slow chorus sweep: pan a pair of heads slowly across the audience while washes sit in a complementary color pair (for example blue and amber). Source [8].
3. Base-look library: build about 20 saved looks, each with color, speed and direction controls you can nudge live (use a speed fader and a color modifier). Source [1].
4. Any-cue-after-any-cue: keep looks in a flat pool of buttons rather than a linear list so you can jump to whichever fits the moment. Source [7].
5. Texture from stillness: hold 1-2 heads fixed on gobo textures while the others move. Source [1].
